How it works

What is learned, how it is trained, how it is scored.

The simulator records the state of a world every 0.01 s: the position and speed of every joint. For a hinge, the position is its angle. A network gets the current state and predicts the next one. Chaining its predictions lets it play the physics forward with no simulator; this is called a rollout.

1-step error

Squared error of one prediction ÷ the size of a typical one-step change. 1.0 means no better than guessing the average change; 0.001 means the error is 0.1% of it. Log scale in the charts.

Valid steps

How many of 100 rollout steps (1 s) pass before the prediction is clearly off: error above 0.5 of the state's typical spread. 100 = good for the whole second.

Shuffle test & gap

Shuffle: scramble one input between test examples. If the error barely changes, the model ignores that input. Gap: test error ÷ training error. Far above 1 means memorised.

Hanging pendulum

Rods hang from a raised mount and swing freely. Nothing touches anything. Default for experiments 3–10: 8 separate pendulums, 16 numbers per state.

Ground pendulum

The project's original setup. Rods are hinged just above a solid floor and start upright with a random push, so they fall, hit the floor and come to rest.

Hanging vs ground pendulum

How much harder is the original ground setup than a freely hanging pendulum?

8 single pendulums in both cases, gravity told, same model and training.

Conclusion

  • The hanging setup is learned almost perfectly: one-step error 0.0005, and all 100 rollout steps stay accurate.
  • The ground setup is about 1,600× harder: error 0.82, barely better than guessing. Rollouts go off after about 25 steps (0.25 s).
  • Ground rods touch the floor in 80% of steps. A breakdown of the errors (seed 0) shows rods lying still are predicted well. The error comes from speeds while a rod falls or bounces, where each impact reverses the speed suddenly.
  • So the hanging pendulum is the clean test case, and all later comparisons use it.

Does the model learn the floor?

Do predicted rods respect the floor, and are the steps with contact the hard part?

Ground setup: 8 single pendulums, and single chains of 2, 4 and 8 links. In the true motion, rods never sink more than about 2 cm into the floor, so more than 5 cm counts as "through the floor".

Conclusion

  • The model does not learn collision physics. In predicted rollouts a rod is more than 5 cm inside the floor in 59% of states for 8 pendulums, and 23–42% for the chains. In the true motion this never happens. On average, the deepest point of a predicted rollout is 27 cm below the floor (68 cm for the 8-link chain).
  • The share grows over the rollout. For 8 pendulums it is 0% at the start, 87% after half a second and 99% after one second. The model learned that rods fall, but nothing stops them at the floor.
  • Steps with floor contact are 1.2–2.4× harder than steps without. But even steps with no contact (0.40–0.64) are hundreds of times worse than the hanging pendulum. The floor hurts the whole model, not only the moment of impact.
  • The model only sees angles and speeds. Where the floor is must be inferred from the data, and a hard limit like that is exactly what a smooth network approximates badly.

More pendulums vs longer chains

Is it the size of the state or the coupling between joints that makes prediction hard, hanging and on the ground?

State size D = 2 × joints. "More pendulums": 1, 2, 4, 8 separate single pendulums. "Longer chain": one pendulum with 1, 2, 4, 8 links. Same D at each point. Solid = hanging, dashed = ground.

Same state size D = 16 in both videos, hanging: 8 joints as separate pendulums (left) vs 8 joints coupled in one chain (right).

Conclusion

  • Hanging, more pendulums: error grows from 0.00002 (D = 2) to 0.0005 (D = 16), and every rollout stays good for all 100 steps. A bigger state alone is easy.
  • Hanging, longer chain: error rises to 0.46 at 8 links, and rollouts last 37 steps. At the same D = 16 that is 900× worse than 8 separate pendulums. Coupled joints are chaotic: small errors grow fast.
  • Ground: everything is hard, even a single pendulum (error 0.59, 39 steps). Size and coupling barely matter there, because the floor dominates.

Flow matching vs regression, change vs next state

Which model and which prediction target work best?

Hanging, 8 pendulums. "Change" = predict the difference to the next state; "next state" = predict the whole next state.

The same test clip for all four, truth on the left of each. Watch the last second: the average angle error after 1 s is 0.8°, 4.4°, 7.9° and 64°.

Conclusion

  • Predict the change: in 0.01 s a pendulum barely moves, so the next state is almost a copy of the current one. Predicting only the change is about 33× more accurate with flow matching.
  • Flow matching beats plain regression by about 16× on the same target. Regression also memorises: its test error is 37× its training error, against 2.7× for flow matching.
  • Regression on the next state is the worst combination: error 0.80, rollouts last 15 steps.

Telling the model gravity

When does it matter whether the model is told the gravity of a clip?

Hanging, 8 pendulums. Gravity is either the same in every clip (9.81) or drawn per clip from 4–15. The model is either given g as an input or not.

Truth on the left of each video. Average angle error after 1 s: 0.6°, 13° and 0.8°.

Conclusion

  • Gravity fixed, not told: no harm at all (error 0.0002). With nothing to tell, the model learns the single gravity from the data.
  • Gravity varies, not told: error 0.085, about 170× worse than when told, and rollouts last 65 steps instead of 100. From one state the model cannot tell the Moon from Jupiter.
  • Told: the model relies on it heavily. Scrambling gravity between test examples makes the error about 220× larger.
  • So telling the model gravity matters exactly when gravity varies across the data.

Gravities outside the training range

Does a model trained on gravity 4–15 work at gravity 2, 20 or 25?

Each trained model is tested on clips at six fixed gravities. The shaded band is the 4–15 training range.

All three models on the same clip with gravity 25, outside the 4–15 range of the first model. Average angle error after 0.5 s: 25°, 1.6° and 86°.

Conclusion

  • Trained on 4–15 and told gravity, the model is excellent inside the range (0.0004 at Earth gravity). Outside it gets steadily worse: 17× at g = 2, 73× at g = 20, 530× at g = 25.
  • Trained on a wider range (2–25), all six test gravities stay good (at most 0.002), at a small cost inside the old range (0.0008 instead of 0.0005).
  • The model interpolates between the gravities it has seen. It has not learned gravity as a rule it can extend. Coverage in the training data is what counts.

Joint damping

Does joint friction that varies between clips hurt, and does telling the model fix it?

Hanging, 8 pendulums, gravity told. Damping is a braking torque proportional to joint speed. At 0.15 it stops a swinging rod within about a second. It is either absent, the same in every clip (0.075), or drawn per clip from 0–0.15.

Conclusion

  • The same pattern as gravity. Damping that is the same in every clip costs little even when not told (error 0.0016, 100 steps).
  • Damping that varies and is hidden makes the error 0.018, about 35× the no-damping case, and rollouts last 80 steps.
  • Telling the model recovers most of it (0.0032, 100 steps), and it uses the input: scrambling damping makes the error 9× larger.

Past states as context

Do 2–5 past states help, and when?

Hanging, 8 pendulums. K = number of states the model sees. "Full" = positions and speeds. "Positions" = angles only, no speeds.

Top: angles only, 1 vs 2 past states. Bottom: gravity not told, 1 vs 5 past states. Average angle error after 1 s: 47°, 11°, 13° and 6°.

Conclusion

  • Full state, gravity told: history adds nothing (≈ 0.0005 for every K). One state already contains everything needed for the next.
  • Positions only: one state is useless (error 1.56, rollouts last 10 steps), because speed cannot be seen in a single snapshot. Two states are enough to work it out (0.0003), and with 3–5 states rollouts last about the full 100 steps.
  • Gravity not told: 5 states cut the one-step error 5× (0.085 → 0.017): the model starts to infer gravity from how fast things accelerate. Rollouts improve only a little (65 → 69 steps), so this is far from telling it gravity.
  • History helps exactly when a single state is missing something: speeds, or a hidden factor like gravity.

Random nuisance tokens

Does the model ignore meaningless random inputs, or learn from them?

Hanging, 8 pendulums. Tokens are random numbers added to the input that have nothing to do with the physics. They are either redrawn every step, or drawn once per clip, which makes them work like a name tag for the clip.

Conclusion

  • Tokens redrawn every step are ignored: error unchanged (0.0005), and scrambling them changes nothing.
  • Tokens fixed per clip are learned from: with 32 of them the error is 3.5× higher, the train/test gap rises from 2.7 to 7, and the model leans less on real gravity: scrambling gravity now raises the error about 60× instead of about 220×. It partly uses the tag to recognise training clips.
  • Without gravity told, 32 per-clip tokens drive the gap to 141 (from 19). The model memorises each training clip, including its gravity, by its tag. On new clips the tags mean nothing, so test error does not improve.

Other worlds

How do springs, cart-poles and bouncing balls compare with the hanging pendulum at the same state size?

All with 16 numbers per state and gravity told. Spring chains: 4 chains of 2 masses on springs. Cart-poles: 4 carts sliding on rails, each with a free pole. Balls: 4 balls bouncing in a box.

Conclusion: which worlds are useful tests

  • Spring chains are the easiest (error 0.00002): smooth, linear motion. Useful as a sanity check, too easy to tell models apart.
  • Cart-poles are a good middle ground (0.03, 97 steps). They mix sliding and swinging joints, and each cart and pole push on each other. One of the 3 seeds was 5× worse than the other two, hence the wide interval.
  • Bouncing balls fail like the ground pendulum (0.41, 32 steps): bounces flip the speed instantly. They are the cleanest test for whether a model can learn contacts, without the extra difficulty of chains.

Scaling the state: 16 to 128 dimensions

How does the model cope as the state grows from D = 16 to 128, with independent joints and with coupled ones?

Hanging, gravity told, the same network and data size as everywhere else. Separate pendulums: 8, 16, 32 and 64 single pendulums. Coupled: 2, 4, 8 and 16 chains of 4 links. Both have D = 16, 32, 64 and 128 numbers per state.

Both at D = 128. Average angle error after 1 s: 15° for the separate pendulums, 66° for the chains.

Conclusion

  • Separate pendulums degrade slowly. The 1-step error grows from 0.0005 at D = 16 to 0.025 at D = 128 (about 50×), but rollouts stay useful: 92 of 100 steps are still valid at D = 128.
  • This is a capacity limit, not memorising. The training error grows just as much and the test/train gap stays near 3. The same 256-unit network has to learn 64 copies of one pendulum separately; it does not know they are identical. It also leans less on gravity as D grows (scrambling gravity raises the error 216× at D = 16, only 3× at D = 128).
  • Coupled chains are bad from the start and get worse. Error 0.24 at D = 16 rising to 0.71 at D = 128; rollouts drop from 66 to 21 valid steps. Even two independent 4-link chains are 3× worse than one (0.074). These models also ignore gravity completely.
  • Growing the state hurts much less than coupling does. At D = 128 the separate pendulums are still about 30× better than the chains. Two natural next steps: a bigger network, or one that shares weights across identical units.

Main takeaways

  1. Smooth physics is easy, at any size. Swinging, springs and gravity are learned well, even with 128 numbers per state.
  2. Coupling and contacts are hard. Chains of joints and collisions with floors or walls are where the model breaks. It never learns a "can't go there" rule.
  3. The model needs to see every factor that varies. A hidden factor that changes between clips (gravity, friction) costs a lot; past snapshots only partly make up for it.
  4. It does not extrapolate. It only works inside the range of conditions it was trained on.
  5. It memorises when given a shortcut. Anything constant within a clip and unique to it becomes a clip ID.
  6. At high D the limit is capacity, not memorising. A fixed-size network learns every identical pendulum separately.

Notes on the setup

  • Light direction is not used. It was considered as an extra input, but it only changes how a rendered image looks. The model takes physical states, never images, so light cannot affect what it learns.
  • Ground geometry. In the first version the hinge sat exactly on the floor, so the rounded end of every rod was always 3 cm inside it, in permanent contact. Hinges now sit 5 cm above the floor, and contacts are stiffer, so true rods stay within about 2 cm of the floor surface.
  • Scale of the study. 47 variants × 3 seeds = 141 trained models. Each has 200 training clips of 2 s, is tested on 50 unseen clips, and uses the same network (3 residual blocks of 256 units, 60 epochs). Numbers would shift with more data or a bigger network; the comparisons are what matter.
  • The ground error breakdown (rods at rest predicted well, falling and bouncing speeds badly) comes from one extra retrained model (seed 0). Everything else is the mean of 3 seeds.

Next steps

  1. Share weights across identical parts. One small network applied to every pendulum or link, with message passing between neighbouring links for chains. Separate pendulums should then cost almost nothing extra as D grows; it may also help chains.
  2. Separate capacity from data at D = 128. Grow the network (256 → 512 → 1024 units) and, separately, the data (200 → 800 clips), to see which one the high-D drop comes from.
  3. Push the dimension higher: D = 256, 512 and 1024 with separate pendulums, towards the original goal of up to 1024 links.
  4. Represent angles as sine and cosine. A raw angle jumps at ±180° although the position is the same; sin/cos removes that artificial jump. Cheap to try on chains and cart-poles, whose poles swing all the way round.
  5. Train on multi-step rollouts. Score training over 5–10 chained predictions, not one step, so the model learns to correct its own drift.
  6. Give the model contact information, such as each rod tip's height above the floor, and test whether ground pendulums and bouncing balls stop sinking through the floor.
